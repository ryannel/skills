# PROPS: the harbour props set

## Brief

Saltwind has one module for this goal, so there is no `ARCHITECTURE.md`. PROPS owns every harbour prop: its mesh,
its collision, its share of the atlas, the atlas itself, the shared prop materials, and the lantern's swing and creak.
Later modules (harbour layout, boat, rendering, audio) consume props through the contract below and own nothing in
`assets/props/`.

## What the player should see, feel and hear

Harbours should feel built by hand by the people who live there. Every prop should look like a slightly wonky,
well-loved object, not a catalogue part. The reference is A Short Hike (adamgryu, 2019): chunky low-poly shapes,
soft colour that is mostly flat with gentle gradients, a small warm palette, and silhouettes that read at a glance
from a distance. What to avoid is the generic low-poly pack: perfect symmetry, ruler-straight planks, identical
bevels on every edge, noisy photo-derived textures, and the clean uniformity of kits like Kenney's Pirate Kit.

The range the props must hold up across:

- **Distance:** 1.5 m (the player walking past), 12 m (sailing alongside the quay), 25 m (approaching the harbour).
  Silhouettes must read at 25 m; details must not fall apart at 1.5 m.
- **Height:** eye height on the dock (about 1.2 m, a small character) and from a boat on the water (about 0.5 m),
  which shows the undersides of the crane jib, the lantern bracket and anything overhanging the water.
- **Light:** day (high warm sun, sky fill) and dusk (low orange sun, the lantern lit).
- **Company:** each prop alone, and all twelve together in one corner of a harbour, where they must read as one set.

The lantern swings gently, as if in a light sea breeze, and creaks in time with its swing. The creak loops forever
without a click, and several lanterns on one quay don't creak in unison.

## Mechanisms

### Props are generated by scripts in Blender, then wrapped in Godot scenes

Agents can't sculpt by hand in an editor, so each prop is built by a Python generator run in headless Blender
(`blender -b -P`), exported as glTF binary, and imported by Godot. A Godot scene per prop wraps the imported mesh and
adds collision and any behaviour. Blender is chosen over building meshes in GDScript because it gives the builder real
modelling operations (bevel, solidify, array, curve, smooth-by-angle, UV tools) and gives the owner files they can open
and hand-tweak later. Godot's own glTF import is the standard path into Godot 4, so nothing custom sits between them.

Generators are the source of truth. Running them again reproduces the committed `.glb`, atlas and audio, which keeps
a builder or the owner from silently hand-editing an output that the next regeneration would overwrite. If the owner
later wants to hand-edit a prop in Blender, that prop's `.blend` becomes its source and it leaves the reproducibility
check; that is the owner's call.

### "Hand-made" is a set of rules applied by a shared helper, not left to taste per prop

Scripted geometry drifts towards the perfectly regular look the owner doesn't want, so the generators share one helper
library that applies these rules, and every prop uses it:

- **Chunky proportions.** Boards, hoops, rims and handles are about 1.2-1.5 times real thickness. Small details that
  wouldn't read at 12 m are left out or exaggerated, never modelled at true scale.
- **Controlled irregularity.** Repeated parts vary (plank widths by about 10-20 %, stave lengths, hoop heights), and
  whole parts lean or twist by 1-4 degrees. Long edges get a gentle low-frequency wobble of a few centimetres. All of
  it comes from a seed fixed per prop, so it is deterministic. Irregularity is in the shapes, never per-vertex noise:
  a silhouette should look wonky, not crumpled.
- **Visible facets.** Low segment counts (6-10 sides for small round parts, up to about 14 for the barrel and buoy).
  Hard edges on angular parts and smooth shading on round ones, split by angle.
- **Colour from the atlas, not geometry.** Each face takes its colour from a palette swatch in the atlas; see below.

The style rules live in the helper's code, with the numbers above as named constants, so later props inherit them and
a reviewer can read them in one place.

### One 512 x 512 atlas made of palette ramps plus a few painted details

The atlas is generated by a script, not painted by hand, and is built from:

- **Palette ramps.** A small palette (about 20-32 colours: weathered woods, rope, iron, rust, sailcloth, paint
  accents, fish, glass glow). Each colour is a short ramp, lighter at one end and darker at the other, so a face's UV
  position along the ramp gives the soft painted gradient A Short Hike uses instead of detailed texture. A face picks a
  colour by where its UVs sit, so two planks of one crate can take neighbouring ramps and look individually painted.
- **Small painted details.** A few regions drawn procedurally: wood grain strokes, rope twist, rust blotches, a crate
  stencil, the lantern glass glow, the net mesh (with transparent holes), the wicker weave.
- **Padding.** Every region is padded and faces' UVs sit well inside their region, so mipmapping at gameplay distance
  doesn't bleed one colour into its neighbour.

Filtering is nearest-neighbour with mipmaps, which keeps swatch edges and painted strokes crisp in the way the
reference's pixelated style suggests, without shimmering at a distance.

### Two shared materials, one texture

- `props_opaque` for everything solid.
- `props_cutout` (alpha scissor, both sides drawn) for the net and the lobster-pot mesh, sampling the same atlas.

Every prop uses only these two materials, so the whole set is one texture and at most two material states.

### The lantern's swing is driven by the creak's playback position

- The creak is a mono WAV made by a generator script in this repo, using the stick-slip friction approach from Andy
  Farnell's *Designing Sound* (MIT Press, 2010, the creaking door practical): an irregular train of tiny slip
  impulses, its rate and strength following the load on the hook, excited through a few resonant filters tuned to
  wood and iron. Its length is exactly one full swing period (about 3-5 s), and it holds creaks near the turnarounds
  of the swing, where the hook reverses direction.
- The WAV loops forward over its whole length. The generator makes the loop seamless by construction (for example by
  building the signal periodically or cross-fading the tail into the head), and a check measures the seam.
- The lantern's swing angle is a function of the audio player's playback position, so the swing and the creak can't
  drift apart however long the game runs. The swing is a pendulum-like ease (slow at the ends, fastest at the bottom)
  of a few degrees, never a linear back-and-forth.
- Each lantern starts at a phase taken from a hash of its world position, so lanterns on one quay are out of step and
  stay deterministic. When audio is unavailable or the player isn't playing (headless, muted, culled), the lantern
  falls back to the same function of game time, so it still swings.
- The sound is an `AudioStreamPlayer3D` on an `SFX` bus that this module adds to the default bus layout. It is
  clearly audible within about 10 m and silent beyond 25 m at most.
- The lantern carries an `OmniLight3D` and emissive glass from the atlas glow region, both off when `lit` is false.

If the owner rejects the synthesised creak at the M2 listening check, the fallback is a CC0 recording they choose,
which is their decision under the profile's licence rule. The swing-from-playback mechanism stays the same either way.

### Collision is primitive

Each prop has a `StaticBody3D` with box, cylinder, capsule or convex shapes that approximate it, never trimesh
collision. Swinging and hanging parts (the lantern body, crane rope and hook, net) have none.

## What this module promises other modules

These are the contract. Changing them needs a proposal settled by the planner.

- **Paths.** Each prop is `res://assets/props/<id>/<id>.tscn`, with `<id>` from the table above. The atlas is
  `res://assets/props/atlas/props_atlas.png`; the materials are `res://assets/props/materials/props_opaque.tres` and
  `props_cutout.tres`.
- **Space.** Metres, +Y up, the prop's front facing -Z. The origin is at the centre of the prop's footprint, at the
  height where it rests on the ground, so dropping it on a dock at y = 0 seats it.
- **Scene shape.** The root is a `Node3D` named after the prop. Collision is a `StaticBody3D` on physics layer 1 with
  primitive or convex shapes only. Instancing a prop has no side effects outside its own subtree.
- **Budget.** At most 1,500 triangles across all meshes in a prop scene, and only the two prop materials.
- **Lantern.** Exposes `lit: bool` (default true) and `creak_volume_db: float`. Plays on the `SFX` bus.
- **Crane.** The jib is its own node, rotating about the mast's vertical axis; the rope and hook hang from the jib
  tip and stay vertical as the jib turns. The crane doesn't animate itself.
- **Rendering.** A future rendering module may replace the materials' shader, provided it keeps sampling albedo (and
  alpha for the cutout) from the atlas by UV0. Props never assume a particular lighting model.

## Tooling the builder makes

- `scripts/build_props.sh`: regenerates the atlas, the creak and every prop.
- `scripts/check.sh <suite>`: the headless checks, with suites `budget`, `atlas`, `contract`, `audio`, `repro` and
  `all`. Each suite exits non-zero on failure and includes a deliberately bad fixture it must reject, so a check that
  passes everything is caught.
- `scripts/capture.sh`: renders lookdev captures (turntables and frame sequences) on the godot-lookdev target.
- `res://lookdev/`: a reference lighting rig (day and dusk) on a simple wooden dock above flat water, standing in for
  the game's lighting until that exists.

## Cost

The only targets are the owner's: 1,500 triangles per prop and one 512 x 512 atlas. Nothing else is budgeted. The
builder records each prop's triangle count, and the set's total, in the M6 catalogue so later budget decisions start
from measurements.

## Assumptions made without the owner

The owner couldn't be reached while planning; these were decided here and are worth a glance.

1. Twelve props, chosen as listed above. The owner said "12ish" and named crates, barrels, bollards, a crane, a
   lantern and nets; the other six are typical harbour dressing.
2. Meshes are scripted in Blender 5.2 (present on the Mac) rather than hand-modelled; Godot 4.4 must be installed,
   since it wasn't found on the machine.
3. The creak is synthesised in the repo rather than sourced, to avoid licence questions; the owner approves it by ear.
4. A second material (alpha cutout) is allowed, as long as it samples the same single atlas. Nets read far better
   with real holes.
5. Nearest-neighbour filtering with mipmaps. The game's own render style (for instance a low-res pixelated render
   like A Short Hike's) belongs to a future rendering module.
6. One version of each prop, no variants. Variants could come later from the same generators with a different seed.
7. The character is small, about 1.2 m eye height, in the spirit of the reference; prop scale is set against that.

## Risks, riskiest first

1. **The look comes out generic.** Scripted geometry tends to regularity. M1 proves the style on two props, with an
   owner sign-off, before ten more copy it.
2. **The creak sounds synthetic or clicks.** M2 proves it with seam measurements, a spectrogram a reviewer can read,
   and the owner's ears.
3. **The crane doesn't fit 1,500 triangles and still read.** M3 builds it early, alone.
4. **Cutout nets vanish or go solid at distance** because of alpha and mipmaps. M4 inspects them at all three
   distances.
