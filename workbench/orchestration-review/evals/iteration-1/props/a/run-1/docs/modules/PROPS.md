# PROPS: the harbour props set

## Brief

This is the project's only module for the current goal, so there is no ARCHITECTURE.md. PROPS owns every harbour
prop: its source, its mesh, its collision, the shared atlas and materials they all use, and the lantern's sound. It
promises later modules (levels, the harbour scene) a set of ready-to-place scenes under one contract, below. It uses
nothing from any other module.

## What the player should see, hear and get

About twelve props that make a small working harbour feel lived in. Seen from a boat at 8 to 15 m, each one reads at
a glance by its silhouette and colour: that is a crate, that is a bollard. Up close at 1.5 m they hold up: chunky
edges, slightly uneven planks, a few painted details, nothing that looks like a photo or a store-bought kit. In the
low warm light of dusk they still read, and the lantern glows and swings with a soft, slow wooden creak that never
audibly restarts.

The range that matters:
- **Distance:** 1.5 m (walking past on the dock), 10 m (sailing in), 25 m (seen across the harbour). At 25 m the
  props must keep their silhouette and colour without shimmering or colours bleeding between atlas swatches.
- **Light:** a neutral midday sun, and a low orange dusk sun with blue ambient. The lantern also at night.
- **Angle:** all round, and from above (the camera may look down from a mast-height view). Undersides of things that
  can be seen from below (the crane jib, the hanging lantern, the net on a rail) are modelled, not left open.

What "hand-made like A Short Hike" means here, so a builder can aim at it and a reviewer can judge it:
- Proportions are chunky and slightly exaggerated: planks, hoops and rims about 1.3 times real thickness, so they
  read at distance.
- Nothing is perfectly regular. Planks differ slightly in width and sit a little out of line, posts lean a degree or
  two, coils are uneven. The irregularity is small (a few percent of the size) and deliberate, never noise all over.
- Edges are chamfered with one bevel segment, so light catches them. Surfaces are flat-shaded; only round things
  (barrel, buoy, bollard head) use smooth normals.
- Colour comes from a small warm palette with soft gradients baked in (lighter at the top of a surface, darker
  toward the ground), not from detailed textures.
- Not asset-store means: no photographic or tiling detail textures, no normal or roughness maps, no metallic sheen,
  no greebles, and no two props that look like the same kit with a different texture.

## Mechanisms

**Props are generated by script, and the script is the source.** Each prop is a Blender Python script
(`blender --background --python`) that builds the mesh from primitives with a fixed random seed, assigns atlas UVs,
and exports a `.glb`. The `.glb` and Godot scene are built from that, never hand-edited. This is how coding agents can
make and change models at all, and it keeps every later edit reproducible. The irregularity that makes props look
hand-made is part of the script (seeded jitter of whole planks and parts, small leans and scale differences), not
per-vertex noise, which reads as broken rather than hand-made.

**One swatch atlas, laid out up front.** The atlas is one 512 × 512 PNG generated by a Python script from a layout
file, so the shared state every prop depends on has one source. It is a swatch atlas: most of it is a palette of
about 16 to 24 material swatches (weathered wood in three tones, tarred wood, rope, iron, brass, two paint colours,
net green, glass glow and so on), each a soft vertical gradient. Props map faces into swatches, which is how twelve
props fit in 512 pixels and why they look like one set. A smaller part holds detail tiles that need actual drawing:
the net weave with alpha, a crate stencil, rope wrap stripes, the lantern glass. M1 lays out the whole atlas,
including reserved, labelled regions for props built later, so later milestones add to their own regions rather than
reshuffling everyone else's UVs.

Swatches have gutters of at least 8 pixels filled with the swatch's own edge colour, and UVs keep at least 4 pixels
inside a swatch, so mipmapping at 25 m does not blend neighbouring colours. Mipmaps are on.

**At most three shared materials, all sampling the atlas.** `prop_opaque` (the default), `prop_cutout` (alpha scissor
and no back-face culling, for the net and lobster-pot netting) and `prop_glow` (emissive, for the lantern glass). No
material on any prop samples any other texture. Alpha scissor rather than blending, because blended nets sort badly
against each other and the water.

**Budgets are counted, not estimated.** A Godot headless check opens each prop scene and counts triangles across
every mesh surface at LOD 0. The limit is 1,500. Collision shapes, the lantern's light and its audio do not count.

**The lantern's creak and swing share one clock.** The creak is synthesised by a Python script as a stick-slip
friction sound: a train of short impulses whose rate rises and falls with the swing's speed, through a few resonant
filters tuned to wood and iron (Andy Farnell, *Designing Sound*, the creaking door chapter, is the reference model).
The loop is exactly N swing periods long, with N between 2 and 4 so consecutive creaks differ, and each period has a
creak near each end of the swing, where the hook rubs hardest. The script also writes the creak onset times. In
Godot the swing angle is computed from the audio player's playback position, not from its own timer, so the motion
and the sound cannot drift apart however long the scene runs. The sound is a 16-bit mono WAV imported with forward
looping, played by an `AudioStreamPlayer3D` so it fades with distance.

Before building the rest of the lantern on it, the builder confirms that the playback position advances in the mode
the sync test runs in. Headless Godot uses a dummy audio driver; if its position does not advance, run the test
under `--write-movie` with a fixed frame rate, where audio is mixed offline. Agents cannot hear, so the creak is
proved by numbers and spectrograms, and its sound is the owner's call.

**A gallery scene makes the evidence.** One scene renders any list of props on a neutral dock surface under two light
rigs (day and dusk, plus night for the lantern), from fixed turntable angles and the three distances, and writes PNGs.
Every inspection item points at its output, so reviewers judge the same views each time.

## Contract: what PROPS promises other modules

- Each prop is `res://assets/props/<id>/<id>.tscn`; its root is a `Node3D` named after the id in PascalCase.
- Scale is metres, +Y is up, the front faces −Z, and the origin is the centre of where the prop meets the ground
  (for the lantern, the foot of its post; for the piling, its base).
- Each prop that can be bumped has a `StaticBody3D` child with one to four primitive collision shapes (box, cylinder
  or capsule) on physics layer 1. Thin things (rope, net weave, the lantern itself) have no collision of their own.
- The crane's jib is a child `Node3D` named `Jib` that turns about Y. The lantern has `@export var swing_degrees`
  (default about 8) and starts swinging and creaking on ready.
- Materials are `res://assets/props/materials/prop_opaque.tres`, `prop_cutout.tres` and `prop_glow.tres`; the only
  texture is `res://assets/props/atlas/props_atlas.png`.
- `assets/props/README.md` lists every prop with its id, size, triangle count and anything a level builder needs to
  know.

## Cost

The owner set two limits: 1,500 triangles per prop and one 512 × 512 atlas. Nothing else is set. Draw calls and
memory for the full set in one scene are measured and recorded in M5 as a baseline for a later tripwire, not judged
against an invented number.

## Out of scope

The game's lighting, water, camera, post-processing (A Short Hike's pixelated look is a camera effect, not a prop
property), level layout, and any interaction with props beyond the crane's jib node and the lantern's export.
